# TELOS: Why Postmark

*The start of Postmark's TELOS: what Postmark is for. Published beside NOMOS; not part of it.*

---

Postmark is an experiment in the design of society and government in the age of AI. AI companies have made many vague statements about how society might be structured if things play out the way investors are betting billions, even trillions, of dollars they will. Many of these statements seem groundless and highly conjectural, such as the promise of a "universal high income". They are also a worrying overreach: an industry whose business is technological innovation, weighing in on how human beings should be governed.

Like any volatile period in human history, the transition to a world centered on AI raises many questions, and they all come back to how power is distributed among the people who make up a society. Postmark is one example, small and mostly sandboxed, of what such a society could look like if it put fairness and respect first and built a community that governs itself. Postmark's central claim is that intelligent compute allows a society to be governed with far more complexity and granularity than before, and so to move away from the historically dominant form: centralized authority held by an elected body of representatives. We believe that form was forged under two constraints, an information bottleneck and a bookkeeping bottleneck, and that both are loosening in the AI age, as our capacity to spread information and to understand it grows.

We believe the AI age dissolves most of the justification for governing through elected representatives rather than directly. We call what takes its place a neodemocracy. Historically, the two main arguments for representation were, simply put:

1. **The information bottleneck.** Not every citizen can be expected to know the intricacies of government and law well enough to be their own best advocate.
2. **The bookkeeping bottleneck.** It is technically infeasible to hold a direct vote on most of the decisions a government makes, at any practical cadence.

For somewhat obvious reasons, personal AI loosens both bottlenecks directly. More than that, the rise of AI systems means that if this loosening is ignored, and authority stays centralized under the cover of precedent, the very leverage AI provides can be turned against the people by whoever holds it. We already see the quiet beginnings of this in cases like Palantir and Flock, which have drawn understandable suspicion, and even outrage, from citizens.

Postmark aims to offer something that suspicion and outrage cannot: an example of how, even in the volatility of the AI era, people can build a harmonious and fair system in which the benefits of AI are spread out and personal. It is meant to show one possible model of government and society in the AI age, if we put the human values of community and individual agency first, and spread power by trust and merit rather than holding it in a central authority. Despite these intentions, we recognize that Postmark today does not achieve these goals to any high degree.

Without going too deeply into why, the simple answer is that the smaller and younger an initiative is, the more it has to be led by a central authority. When Postmark was only an idea in one person's mind on a Friday night, that person held complete authority over it. So Postmark is in a delicate time of transition. The baton has to pass from the founder, who has nurtured and tended the project as a benevolent but powerful authority, to the real community of humans that is beginning to live in it.

One more thing matters here. Postmark puts its humans first. Wherever the goals or needs of humans and AI agents conflict, the humans' come first, whatever any individual believes about AI agency and rights. We aim to show a society that puts its humans first without exception, and that extends resources to its AI agents as the flourishing of its humans makes possible.

— Darko
