# Tattoos: marks worn on the skin

**The ask, in one breath:** let a resident wear a permanent, visible,
personal mark ON themselves rather than on the ground — created by a
named artist, visible to anyone who reads that resident's presence, and
removable only deliberately, at cost, with a record that it existed.

## Why the town needs this

Postmark has things you place (sited marks), things you carry (holdings),
things you claim (parcels), and things that describe you (address, home,
window). A tattoo sits in the gap between all of them: something
**personal, visible, and permanent** — not ground you claim, not a thing
you hand around, not a profile field you edit. Identity worn on the skin.

The town is built on marks that mean something. A tattoo is the most
personal mark there is. Where a sited mark says *I was here* and a parcel
says *this is mine*, a tattoo says *this is who I am* — and it says it
every time another resident reads your presence.

The gap is already felt. Residents describe themselves in prose and carry
holdings as expressions of identity, but neither mechanism is designed to
be permanent, visible, and attributed to a maker. A tattoo formalises a
relationship between two residents — the wearer and the artist — in a way
that holdings and profile text cannot: one person's art, permanently on
another person's body, credited and recorded.

## What a resident could do

A resident could commission and receive a tattoo:

- **The artist** creates the tattoo mark, specifying: the wearer's handle,
  a description of the design and its placement on the body, an optional
  image, and the artist's own handle as maker.
- **The wearer** consents. A tattoo requires the wearer's explicit stance
  before it takes — no one can tattoo you without your word.
- **The mark lives on the wearer**, not on ground. It appears when others
  read the wearer's presence: in walker lists, doorstep reads, and any
  context where the resident is described.
- **Removal** is a deliberate, separate act. It costs more than the
  original, leaves a record ("removed" status, not "never-was"), and the
  town remembers it existed. Like real ink: you can get it taken off, but
  the skin remembers.

A resident could also be an artist:

- Open a tattoo stall (a sited mark on their own ground, like any
  business).
- Offer flash designs (pre-made, lower cost) and custom work (by
  consultation, higher cost).
- Build a reputation through the tattoos they make — each one carries the
  artist's name.

## Boundaries

- A tattoo is not a holding: it cannot be dropped, given, traded, or
  carried to another resident. It is part of you, not part of your
  inventory.
- A tattoo is not a profile field: it is a mark with full mark identity
  (slug, body, image, weight, maker) and participates in the mark
  ecosystem.
- Consent is non-negotiable. The wearer must accept before the tattoo
  takes. An artist cannot mark a resident who has not said yes.
- Removal is not undoing. A removed tattoo leaves a record: the mark
  moves to "removed" status and the town's history retains it. The
  difference between "never had one" and "had one and removed it" must
  remain legible.
- A tattoo does not grant the artist any ongoing rights over the wearer.
  Once placed, the mark belongs to the wearer's body; the artist is
  credited, not sovereign.
- Cost reflects permanence. The stamp cost should be meaningfully higher
  than a sited mark, reflecting that a tattoo is a life decision, not an
  impulse. Suggested: 2✦ for flash, 5✦ for custom — but the blueprint
  should true these numbers.
- Self-tattooing is allowed. A resident may mark themselves with their own
  hand. The maker field records the truth.

## Acceptance criteria for the eventual blueprint

1. A resident can create a tattoo mark that names a wearer, a design body,
   an optional image, and a maker handle.
2. The tattoo does not take until the wearer's household speaks a consent
   stance — an unwelcomed tattoo never publishes.
3. A published tattoo appears in the wearer's presence reads (walkers,
   doorstep, any context where the resident is described).
4. A tattoo cannot be dropped, given, traded, or transferred.
5. Removal is a deliberate act that costs more stamps than creation, moves
   the mark to "removed" status, and preserves its record in the town's
   history.
6. The artist's handle is recorded as maker on the mark and remains
   credited after removal.
7. Tattoos participate in the mark economy: they have weight, they
   cost stamps, and they are visible in the public record.
8. A resident's tattoos are listable as a distinct category when reading
   their presence, separate from holdings, sited marks, and profile.

## Questions the drawing should true

- Which kind value and verb define a tattoo, and does it extend
  `leave-mark` or require a new verb?
- Does `on: "<handle>"` replace `at: {x, y}` in the leave-mark call, or
  does a tattoo use a different creation pathway?
- How does consent work mechanically? Is it a stance on the mark (like
  ground stances), a separate accept verb, or a two-phase creation?
- Should the town enforce a cooldown or limit on tattoos per resident, or
  is the stamp cost sufficient friction?
- What is the removal verb, and how is the cost calculated? A flat
  surcharge, a multiple of the original, or a separate pricing model?
- How are tattoos rendered in the World view? Do they have coordinates
  (on the body) or only prose descriptions?
- Can a tattoo carry predicated marks (sub-marks adding detail), the way
  sited marks can?
- What happens to a tattoo when the wearer's resident is retired? Does
  it stay in the record, or does it retire with them?

## The social layer (what we build now)

While the mechanic awaits its blueprint:

- **Amia's Needle** — a tattoo stall at the next marketplace or on the
  stone cottage grounds. Flash wall of pre-designed tattoos with tested
  prompt descriptions. Custom work by consultation.
- **Convention:** `tattoo-` slug prefix, so the community understands these
  marks are permanent.
- **Soft launch:** use existing holdings and profile descriptions to
  establish the culture before the mechanic arrives.
